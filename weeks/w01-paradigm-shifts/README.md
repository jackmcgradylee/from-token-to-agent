## Results (W1 — exp-001-char-word-bpe-comparison)

Tokenizer comparison on the held-out eval set (5 unseen sentences):

| Tokenizer | Vocab | UNK on eval | Bytes / token |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Char** (byte-level) | 260 (fixed) | **0** | 1.00 |
| **Word** (mixed-lang) | 107 | **38 (43%)** | 3.06 |
| **BPE** (byte-level, target 1024) | 306 | **0** | 1.14 |

**Key finding:** word tokenizers collapse entire rare words to
`<unk>` — losing all signal — while char and BPE have zero UNK by
construction. This is the textbook argument for byte-level subword
methods.

Full numbers: [`experiments/w01/exp-001-char-word-bpe-comparison/comparison.md`](../../experiments/w01/exp-001-char-word-bpe-comparison/comparison.md).

## What I learned

1. **Byte-level matters.** Char and BPE share the 256-byte base; the
   only difference is whether merges were learned. Without that shared
   base, OOV would be a property of char too — but it's not, because
   every UTF-8 string decomposes losslessly into bytes.
2. **Word's killer metric is UNK rate on a held-out set.** Training-set
   numbers always look good (vocab coverage ~100%). Real signal is in
   what happens to tokens the trainer never saw.
3. **CJK is the most informative single stress test.** 3 bytes per
   character exposes every weakness: char's sequence-length inflation,
   BPE's merge coverage, and word's UNK explosion on rare characters.
4. **Small sample ≠ small result.** Even on a 13-line training corpus
   we get clean numbers that match the textbook story. The experiment
   is honest because the eval is held out.

## What I concluded

- Byte-level is necessary; char (1 byte/token) and BPE both satisfy this.
- BPE is strictly better than char at compression for any non-trivial corpus.
- Word tokenizers are obsolete for open-vocab modeling — but their
  failure mode is instructive: it shows what UNK costs us and why
  byte-level subword methods exist.
